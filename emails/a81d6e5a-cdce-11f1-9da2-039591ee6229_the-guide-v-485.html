+ Palestine +

+ Music Is Not Separate From Politics [Sama' Abdulhadi] "I have decided not to participate in Amsterdam Dance Event this week. I had been invited to speak on a panel about 'music and resistance' but it would be disingenuous for me to talk about resistance while, within the same festival's framework, a large number of Israeli artists continue to perform internationally as though nothing is happening. [...] There are artists performing at ADE this week who have served in the Israeli military. Some have publicly expressed support for Israel and its military. Others have remained silent. To platform such artists is to normalize the Israeli state and its crimes.

Festivals, promoters and cultural institutions make choices about who they platform, whose voices they amplify and what they are willing to associate their names with. Those choices are political and they have consequences.

I don't want to be invited to ADE as the token Palestinian voice to talk about resistance and genocide, while the wider programme carries on as planned. I will not allow Palestinian suffering to be reduced to a panel discussion that allows everyone else to return to business as usual afterwards.

We don't need Palestinians to be given a microphone while the industry avoids asking itself difficult questions. We need the industry to take responsibility for what it platforms."

+ Enshittification +

+ Spinning on Empty: Arielle Zuckerberg and the DJs of Late Capitalism [Mother Jones] "Whether it’s Paris Hilton pouting from behind the decks or Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon swaglessly pantomiming at corporate functions, the contemporary DJ has mutated from a countercultural icon to a monolithic archetype that attracts opportunists and narcissists of all stripes.

The “DJ clip,” particularly on the Zuckerberg-owned Instagram, plays a very specific role. Rather than communicate anything of substance about your tastes, values, or artistry, it functions more like a placeholder—something that most people won’t watch for more than a few seconds, but might remember later when they vaguely associate you in their heads under the category of “DJ.” Sometimes, friends will ask me to “engage” with their DJ content to boost its priority with the algorithm, the hope being that if enough people see it, more gigs will come their way.

These algorithmic incentives and the addictive platforms that create them can bring out the worst in people, particularly the overly-thirsty “content creators” who are desperately striving to capitalize on their image as a DJ. I frequently encounter career-oriented social climbers who seem to view DJing and clubs as a networking tool—a means to build their online following through proximity to cultural capital."

+ Instagram Is Not My Job [The music world’s growing embrace of social media and content creation has diluted the discourse, cultivated a lot of bad habits and normalized a worrying amount of unpaid labor] [First Floor] "In an effort to maintain relevance and “meet people where they are,” the music commentariat—journalists, publications and content creators—has inadvertently cultivated an online ecosystem in which it’s generally expected that content will be free and easy to digest, and attempts to encourage the public to leave the confines of social media are either widely ignored or viewed with suspicion.

How that content gets made—or who’s paying for it—is of little concern to most audiences, and since they have little faith in what “creators” have to say to begin with, they have few qualms about blithely trashing anyone whose posts or videos don’t immediately align with their own personal worldview. It’s a miserable system littered with throwaway content and bizarrely entitled know-nothings, and yet pretty much everyone feels trapped within it, seeing Instagram and TikTok as the only potential path toward what’s become an exceedingly rare commodity: visibility."

+ Slop Fatigue Is Already Happening [A torrent of AI-generated garbage is negatively affecting the music industry] [First Floor] "As 5Mag editor Terry Matthew explains in a fiery new column, his publication is being overwhelmed by AI-generated event listings, most of them created not by actual promoters, but scammy operations that spam the internet with listings that “substitute their own affiliate links to expensive tickets on reseller platforms like StubHub in place of the legitimate ticket link from the venue, which has ample and cheaper tickets available.” This leads to a situation in which “it actually takes more time for our staff to suss out a scam event than it does to post a real one.”

+ D.C. +

+ Black Techno Matters [Bernard Farley on seven years of building community] [Music, After All] Techno emerged from a very different social, technological and musical context more than forty years ago. Looking at what is happening now, do you see any meaningful parallels with earlier generations of Black American techno, or does this moment need to be understood on its own terms?

"I think that fearlessness is returning especially as more black artists find each other. I think society needs it more than ever. Part of Techno’s history is its connection to Detroit’s technological industry and its impact on black culture. The technology has changed from automobiles to algorithms, and from assembly lines to Amazon warehouses with robots, but the nature of the oppressive elements remains for black people. The need to feel liberated and free, the need to play the machine instead of being played by it. And I think many artists find power in not only realising that techno is part of our cultural heritage, but that it can also be whatever we want it to be. I think you can feel this in the room when black electronic music artists gather."
