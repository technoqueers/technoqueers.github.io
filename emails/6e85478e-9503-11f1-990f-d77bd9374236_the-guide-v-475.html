+ Habibi City +
[New York Magazine]

+ "Habibi City" reduces Gaza to a backdrop for parties and fine dining [The News Movement] "Habibi City has rightly been praised for giving an underserved group rare mainstream attention. But it only tells a narrow story of SWANA New York. The issue reframes decades of working class communities as a newly emerging "scene," defined by a "thriving party circuit" and "upscale SWANA restaurants" and describes this scene as "born out of the pain of the genocide in Gaza." In doing so, it risks flattening the population it intends to celebrate." 

"The question now is whether being seen will become collective power, and whether this "soft power" in the form of cultural clout will mean less bombs dropping overseas."

+ Habibi City [A cultural shift is underway, led by a generation of New Yorkers coming into its own] “The white boys saying mashallah vibe has really been at an all-time high over the last two years.” While it has all been beautiful to witness, none of this means SWANA New Yorkers don’t still face xenophobia, Islamophobia, and Zionism — along with the repercussions of speaking out against them.

+ Dance Revolution [As the SWANA party scene has grown, so have the ways people choose to gather] "Omar Ahmad, a Palestinian American composer and DJ who now steers Palestine Forever’s residency at the Lot Radio, recalls that, at one sold-out show co-hosted with Nowadays in February 2024, the line to get in stretched several blocks. Ahmad, who also works in emergency medicine, played the headlining set, which included an experimental performance with audio of screaming Gazans during one strike on the Al-Shifa hospital emanating from the venue’s four-point sound system. His sets foreground catharsis over joy and making people “feel seen while still being able to process and release through their bodies.”

+ SWANA Social Club [We threw a party and invited 26 of the artists, chefs, DJs, and others shaping the scene] "Mohammed Iman Fayaz, 35, raised in Queens by Indian immigrants, was 22 when he joined two friends and launched a roving party series, Papi Juice, with the mission of affirming and celebrating trans and queer people of color." 

"DJ Carmen Sandiego, 38, with her friend DJ Myyuh,  created Haza, a long-running party series that was among the first in the city to bring Arabic sounds to the fore."

"Nadim Maghzal, 41, is the Beirut-born DJ behind Laylit, a party he and his co-founders, Saphe Shamoun and Philippe Manasseh, first hosted in 2018."

+ When Eating Out Is Political "Jumana Bishara, co-owner of Palestinian restaurant Tanoreen, remembers the “Mediterranean” days, when it felt like going vague was the only way to get people in the door. “After October 7, people were coming here to specifically support a Palestinian restaurant. It was the only positive thing against the backdrop of the genocide.”

"Many new Israeli restaurants are describing themselves as “Mediterranean.” There could be many reasons why. Perhaps there’s a fear of being targeted. Or they’re second-guessing if they should call their food “Israeli” at all. Regardless of intentions, many people told me that now when they see a restaurant labeled “Mediterranean,” or even “Middle Eastern,” they automatically assume the lack of specificity means it might be Israeli."

"We are asserting our nationalities more, and they’re trying to associate with theirs less because their state is genocidal."

+ Bathhouses +

+ NYC Could Soon Legalize Gay Bathhouses [Sniffies] 
“Think of a refined version of Steamworks but with the energy of Berghain. It will be unlike literally anything New York has seen before.”

A few boner-killing complications stand in the way. First: the remaining AIDS-era state liquor law withholds liquor licenses from venues that facilitate “sexual acts, lewd conduct, and public exposure.” Drinks sales will be a financial necessity for most bathhouses, so a lack of a liquor license presents a real issue.
 
The second hurdle is the archaic regulation and zoning codes of New York City itself. Since the city doesn't currently recognize commercial bathhouse models, an interagency task force will have to rewrite numerous city-level codes. Both problems will have to be solved before we enter our bathhouse cruising golden era.

+ The Music Festival Trying to Make Drug Use Safer [At Shambhala, free testing offers peace of mind] [NYT via archive] "Drug testing of varying kinds has been part of music festivals for years, but few have open and extensive operations like Shambhala — and even fewer, if any, in the U.S. One reason is a 2003 law, the Illicit Drug Anti-Proliferation Act, known as the RAVE Act, which can penalize operators of music festivals for knowingly allowing drug use."

They were frustrated by nightlife spaces marketed toward LGBTQ+ people that weren’t queer-owned, prioritized profit over experience or lacked personality. Together with Nick Eskelinen and Tony Kelley, they built Animal in Brooklyn around a different idea: a bar that also hosts queer markets, fundraisers, art events and gatherings that bring people together outside traditional club hours."
