+ Eris Drew & Octo Octa +

+ Eris Drew and Octo Octa bring pastoralism to big beat on Alchemical Sisters [Fader] “We’ve Seen Great Power” is the second track. The voice at the beginning is a famous singer from the ’60s being interviewed. She’s asked about the hippie movement and whether society is going to change in response to what young people want. She says, essentially, “Yeah, but there’s always going to be a whole big group of straight people who aren’t going for it.”

There’s tremendous pushback against trans liberation, queer liberation, freedom, personal dignity, peace, and treating the world well. “Why Not Dream?” is basically saying that we have to keep our dreams alive and keep dreaming collectively. We have to think about our future, or other people will write it for us—and they’re not going for it."

+ Octo Octa and Eris Drew are better living through alchemy [No Tags] "No Way Back is a true rave, in the sense that there’s an ambient room there. It’s technically a sort of burner theatre, but it’s a warehouse vibe. It’s really like the raves I would go to in the ’90s. It’s technically not licensed for this kind of thing, and it’s going all night, and it’s very, very psychedelic."

"We listened to the No Tags about festival culture, and it was so interesting on so many levels. I really loved the conversation. We actually very intentionally do not call System Activate a festival, and it’s something we correct people on."

+ History +

+ What was it like to DJ in the 1980s? [Pioneer] "In the first installment of this series, we learned how DJs of the 1970s created the foundations for modern DJ culture. As the '80s progressed there were monumental shifts, which can be traced back to the advent of hip-hop (which itself was originally influenced by Jamaican sound systems), the birth of house and techno, and technological advancements in DJ gear. By the end of the '80s, the DJ had gone from the person in the corner no one cared much about, to the centre of attention in what was becoming a global movement."

+ A History of Goth Music in Ten Tracks [RA] "For Minimal Wave and Cititrax founder Veronica Vasicka, goth is less a question of genre than of atmosphere—think hypnotic basslines, cavernous echoes, reverb-soaked guitars and a sense of theatricality and dread. Much of what gets labelled goth, she said, borrows the look without the feeling: "Marilyn Manson isn't goth, nor are nu-metal, emo or pop rock bands automatically goth because they wear black eyeliner." Goth's roots stretch from The Doors, The Velvet Underground and Nico to the post-punk experiments of Bauhaus, Siouxsie and the Banshees and The Cure. And its influence runs through darkwave, cold wave, industrial, EBM and various other strains of contemporary electronic music."
+ R.I.P. +

+ Juan Atkins, techno pioneer and Belleville Three member, dies aged 64 [RA] "Atkins was responsible for giving the genre its name. His track "Techno Music" appeared on the seminal 1988 UK compilation Techno! The New Dance Sound Of Detroit, the release that introduced the Motor City sound to European audiences and fixed the genre's name. Nearly four decades on, the word, and the futurist vision behind it, remain inseparable from the man who first put them to record."

"For Atkins, there was more to his constant songwriting than just making people get lost in the trance of dancing. “My lyrics are supposed to make you think about something other than shaking your booty on the dancefloor and falling in love,” he recently told The Guardian. “I feel like artists have an obligation to say something meaningful.” [Pitchfork]

+ Remembering My Father, Juan Atkins [In her own words, Milan Ariel Atkins shares personal and musical memories of her late father—and how she plans to carry his extraordinary legacy forward.] [RA] "The greatest advice he ever gave me when I started DJing was to just have fun—that energy emits regardless of what equipment you use or what tracks you play." [...] "My father always said he'd continue to create music until he left this planet, and probably after that."

+ Juan Atkins and the Institutional Failures of American Culture [The Detroit techno icon’s untimely death has provoked an outpouring of grief, but it ought to spark questions about why his legacy isn’t being recognized outside the usual electronic music bubble.] [First Floor]

"Techno’s humble origins will forever be part of the genre’s allure, and the (admittedly oversimplified) tale of young Black teenagers creating futuristic sounds amid the post-industrial decay of 1980s Detroit has proven intoxicating to multiple generations of electronic music aficionados.

Several decades later, that tale now sits at the foundation of a global, multibillion-dollar industry, sustaining a vast ecosystem of festivals, clubs, labels, distributors, record stores, publicists, promoters, publications, journalists, influencers and artists—a handful of which have achieved legitimate superstar status. Atkins’ vision spawned a global empire that has not only attracted countless brands, but in recent years also the attention of venture capitalists, private equity firms and multinational corporations.

The lineage of African-American musical genius is long and impressive, yet Juan Atkins and his contemporaries are rarely acknowledged as being a part of it. Electronic music, and especially dance music, is still widely regarded by Americans as a European phenomenon, a tendency that dates back to the late ’90s, when the “electronica” sounds of The Prodigy, Fatboy Slim, Daft Punk, Chemical Brothers and countless others were being actively marketed to “alternative”-leaning suburban youth as a hot new trend from overseas.

The average person, or even the average curator, might find it ridiculous to compare Juan Atkins to someone like Paul McCartney or Mick Jagger, but the role he played in not just electronic music history, but the history of all music, was just as essential."

+ Grief Is Not Enough [First Floor] "Electronic music is virtually everywhere, and its most successful figures are living in luxury—and then flaunting their wealth and privilege online as a marketing tactic. Meanwhile, many of the artists who laid the foundations of house and techno are barely scraping by; there’s no pension plan for DJs, and in the streaming era, even the producers who made legitimate anthems back in the day can only hope to collect a pittance from royalties.

Entering their sixties in a country without public health care, they’re coping with chronic illnesses and the usual maladies that come with age, and rather than settling into their supposed golden years, they’re putting themselves at risk as they angle for scattered gigs in which they’re paid a fraction of what contemporary headliners get and often end up five lines down on an overclogged festival flyer.

By any definition, this is not what “honoring our elders” ought to look like."

He continued to perform internationally until recently, connecting generations of club-goers through his distinctive house sound."
