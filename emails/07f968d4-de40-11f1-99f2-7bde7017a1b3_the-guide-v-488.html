+ Juan Atkins +

+ Juan Atkins Conjured Techno From The Wreckage of American Capitalism [The late techno pioneer’s legacy was more than just a musical revolution — it imagined technology as a tool of unity and liberation, not something that disrupts our lives.] [Audiofemme] 

"Most electronic dance music is now consumed via Spotify playlists, sold-out festivals, and 15-second DJ clips on social media. Music journalism and criticism have been replaced by algorithm-chasing influencers, many of whom seem to have no taste or interest in music except as a way to aurafarm for Instagram followers. Digital streaming and social media platforms have incentivized what author Liz Pelly describes as a “listening crisis,” where music and art become the equivalent of wallpaper — a form of mood-optimized aesthetic filler that has become fully abstracted from the people and processes that create it.

And yet, in the true underground spaces where techno still thrives, Atkins’ legacy has proven the music to be inseparable from the artists, producers, and communities who build culture around shared values. Far from the spectacle of corporate festival stages and the extractive gaze of online content creators, these are spaces where people come to be truly embodied and present with the sound. The spirit of techno is alive and well on phone-less dance floors and in unlisted, semi-legal venues, sustained by local scenes that prioritize DIY community and IRL connections over the addictive compulsions of platform-based algorithmic capture. 
    
The fact that many people are just now hearing Atkins’ name for the first time is scathing evidence of the racism and commercial exploitation that has come to define mainstream electronic dance music in the years that followed. From attempts to claim that techno originated from Germany, to the multi-billion dollar festival industry that sanitized, whitewashed, and rebranded it for mass audiences under the banner of “EDM,” techno pioneers like Atkins have been under-recognized because of the radical histories their work represents."

+ Plenty of Guilt to Go Around [First Floor] "I’m not going to try to list all of the Juan Atkins tributes that are out there. There are so many at this point—even The New York Times finally got its act together late last week, enlisting veteran writer Mike Rubin to put together a list of 10 essential productions from the late techno originator. The similarly formatted Pitchfork round-up, which includes six Atkins productions selected by Gabriel Szatan, goes much deeper. And for the real heads: the latest edition of the Herb Sundays newsletter."

"There’s no amount of effusive praise and Detroit worship which can obscure the fact that the people who first dreamt up and created this music are not the ones who’ve primarily benefitted from its success. That injustice is both borderless and ongoing, and while the loss of Atkins has once again brought the matter into focus, it ought to be prompting questions of what we’re all going to do about it. The answer, as of this moment, appears to be nothing, all but ensuring that the next time a pioneering figure is lost arguably decades too soon, the electronic music realm will be repeating this whole routine."

+ Berlin +

+ Why are we scared of darkrooms? [Inside DTF, the Berlin-based collective redesigning desire for all genders] [Pulp] "DTF is an all-gender space. We don’t want to control who’s allowed in because we don’t want people inside spending their time making assumptions about who does and does not belong, being territorial. It’s a clear departure from the FLINTA model. If you’re down to explore in a space that centers queer desire, it’s a space for you."

“Gay men, God bless them, can have sex standing up or kneeling. They can have sex in minimal darkrooms and improvised cruising spaces. But for everyone else, you might need a space to lie back or sit.” That means mattresses, cushions at multiple levels so bodies can find their own configurations.

"One of DTF’s ambitions is to fuse different sex cultures into the same room, and put people in a space where they’re humble enough to learn from each other."

“DTF is about enjoyment and hedonism. But there’s something very political about these spaces too. Darkrooms are places of contact. We really like Samuel Delany’s Times Square Red, Times Square Blue, describing the Times Square porn theaters as fostering inter-class, interracial, intercultural contact between people who otherwise stay in very separate parts of their society.”
