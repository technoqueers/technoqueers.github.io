+ Bears x Ozempic +

+ Gay bear culture survived body fascism. It can survive Ozempic [Advocate] "Historian Les Wright, who became a bear in the ‘80s when he aged out of being a “Castro twink” and gained weight after he quit smoking cigarettes, has been documenting the bear community since its inception, when older, larger, and hairier gay men were ostracized by the mainstream gay community and didn’t have a place where they were sexually viable.

In 1979, George Mazzei put a name to this subset of chubby and hairy gay men in an article for The Advocate that categorized different parts of the LGBTQ+ community by animals, titled Who's Who at the Zoo? The term gained popularity and was being commonly used in social and sexual gatherings by the early 1980s.

“My body naturally started gaining weight, and as I worked through accepting where my body was going, the bears were there for me,” said Wright, who founded the nonprofit Bear History Project and is working on editing his third book in the seminal bear history book series The Bear Book, which has been tracking the evolution of the gay subculture since 1997."

+ Music Journalism +

+ When Did Music Journalism Get So Sad? [First Floor] "I’ve noticed the emergence of a new type of music journalist: the wistful mourner. Most of these figures are indeed “older,” or are at least old enough to remember a time before all music writing became “content” and talk of the algorithm became paramount. And though they do approach the craft with a certain level of anger, that anger is directed not at the proverbial “kids” and how they’re supposedly doing music wrong, but the unaccountable corporate platforms and unchecked capitalism that has completely upended the culture industries during the past two decades."

+ The Latest Elegy for Music Journalism and Middle-Class Musicianhood [Hearing Things] "I was on a panel with some Gen Z music bloggers. Most of them admitted to accepting money for featuring songs in their TikTok and Instagram videos—the condition being that they have to actually like the song, in which case it’s easy money and mutually beneficial. I view this as payola; they view it as making it work however they can. In some ways I admire their practicality, and in others I think it’s another death knell for criticism.

Millennials got a taste of the old boom times before it was gone for good. Gen Z grew up in a cultural void, so I can’t really blame them for throwing out the rulebook."

+ TYGAPAW +

+ The People Need to be Empowered: An Interview with TYGAPAW [The Quietus] “I discovered through YouTube documentaries that techno originated in Detroit. Once you find out that techno is created by Black artists, it’s like: “that’s why it calls to me”. Rhythmically it makes sense to us in our bodies. House music and techno are really easy to move to. You could hear the Blackness in it, but techno was always whitewashed."

"In 2014, McKenzie created Fake Accent, their own inclusive Black queer space for techno in New York. At these events a sound was nurtured that was focused on experimental music as well as queer, Black club music, and was “operating in the space of queerness that wasn’t palatable, queerness that was undefinable.”

“My favourite genres of music are the ones you cannot have without the movement, without the bodies, without the people. Techno is the dancers. You cannot play techno to an empty room, what would be the point?”

"McKenzie wires their urge to move into their productions, and actively draws from their heritage, as they “infuse [their] ancestry into the sound.” Once that is inserted into the music, “it’s really hard for your body to ignore the call to move,” McKenzie says. “It is the practice of body liberation and mind liberation. If your body is liberated your mind is liberated and you can feel an ultimate release.”
