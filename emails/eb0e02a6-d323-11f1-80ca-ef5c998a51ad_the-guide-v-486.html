+ Receiving the Transmission [Across the country, trans women are thriving in nightlife as DJs, promoters, and even business owners, despite enduring a campaign of hate. What does it take to keep yourself—and everyone else—dancing when so many want you to disappear?] [Slate]

"In the 90s, trans women were most visible in nightlife “as the muse, as the go-go dancer, as the girl at the door that tells people where they can come in,” said Juliana Huxtable, a DJ, artist, and writer from New York. Huxtable herself started out this way, as a hostess and dancer, years before she would headline bills as a DJ. It wasn’t until feminists began pushing for more women in electronic music—and certain parties began to court a queerer population, as opposed to just gay men—that Huxtable felt “invited to participate as a cultural producer,” as opposed to “the interesting, eccentric trans girl that people think is really cool and want at the party, but is not part of the mechanisms and technology of how the party functions.”

"To Huxtable, dance music is a particularly inviting art form for trans people. On a dark, crowded dance floor, you’re apt to find less sexual shame and more gender experimentation than in the world outside. Also, between the lighting, sound systems, and electronic music production, the club is a tech-heavy space. “Historically, there’s been a strong affiliation between trans people and technology,” Huxtable said, nodding to the medical interventions of gender-affirming healthcare. “The metaphor of the cyborg and all of that is super relevant.”

"In a thorough survey of the phenomenon published in Pitchfork a few years ago, Delilah Friedler wrote, “so swiftly and soundly have the tides turned that some call this era ‘the doll takeover.’ ”

+ Montreal DIY venue Parquette closes [RA] "Founded in March 2025, the project converted a former CrossFit studio into a multifunctional event space featuring a gymnasium-style dance floor equipped with Danley stacks. The 7,000-square-foot venue also included a cafe, mezzanine lounge and garden overlooking its namesake Père-Marquette Park. Parquette began as a pilot project associated with FANTOM, a nonprofit supporting queer, trans, BIPOC and all nightlife initiatives. Aside from parties, the space hosted a rich variety of community-focused gatherings including art shows, queer speed dating and self-defence classes for marginalised groups."

+ These photos offer a candid look at NYC’s erotic gay dancers [Slava Mogutin’s new exhibition, NYC GO-GO 2.0, revisits images from his cult photo book depicting male go-go performers partying backstage in the 00s] [Dazed]
 
"NYC Go-Go documents a pre-smartphone world when the internet was in its infancy. “I’m not interested in romanticising the past. There was plenty of exploitation, criminality, infighting and political bullshit back then, too. But revisiting these photographs reminded me of the beauty of being physically present in the world, without documenting every moment for an audience or an algorithm. You went out, met strangers, got into trouble, and sometimes something unexpected and magical happened. You couldn’t manufacture that kind of experience on demand.”

It was a sanctuary where people could go be their authentic selves,” says photographer William Howell, who has just published a collection of scenes from the era in West Side Highway (Dark Entries Editions)."
