+ Israel’s Rave Music Drowns Out the Sound of its Victims [For decades, a combination of EDM and drug culture have helped to numb the horror of occupation, apartheid, and genocide] [Current Affairs] "It would be dishonest to speak of Chicago house music without some understanding of land theft, racial segregation, environmental neglect, and wealth inequality in the North American Great Plains. The same is true of Israeli rave and EDM. Culture never develops in isolation, and electronic music is a reflection of the political systems—in this case, colonization—that allows it to thrive."

"Ravers speak of P.L.U.R. (a hippie acronym standing for “peace, love, unity, and respect”) but their lives are built around a decision to look away from the yawning brutalities of the imperial order, whether in India or Palestine. This denial is so privileged and irresponsible that it tends to align with whiteness and wealth. Goa overflows with Israeli ravers who look inside themselves, ignore the world around them, and elect to obliterate their senses, all as they listen to psychedelic trance."

+ Tblisi +

+ Tbilisi: An Electronic Underground Under Threat [Cold] "The “underground” is difficult to hold onto. Tbilisi’s nightclubs, such as Bassiani, have become some of the most visible techno destinations in the past decade. But they are simultaneously hideouts, home to queer communities and political activists that take shelter from clashes with riot police and socio-normativity in Georgia’s subterranean cracks. Can underground culture remain “underground” once it gains global exposure?

"Tourists often arrive in Tbilisi carrying pre-existing expectations of what its nightlife should be. They come searching for a misinformed image that they have encountered online. But cultural spaces rarely conform to the narratives projected onto them. They depend on shared norms and values, on shared input and participation, relying on a sense of unified and impassioned feeling stemming from a mutual source. New entrants who don’t understand these norms and cannot associate with these Tbilisi-rooted feelings can alter the social character of a space. Visitorial expectations themselves begin to reshape the culture they came to witness and consume."
