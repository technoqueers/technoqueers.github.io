+ First Floor +

+ Underground Acquiescence [Thoughts on San Francisco’s Portola festival, the state of the “underground” and what happens when independent music scenes fail to stand up to creeping corporate interests] "After a decade-plus of the dance music world considering and debating a wide range of topics—usually online, and frequently in relation to big-picture social and political issues like corporate consolidation, gender representation, sexual assault and Palestine—it increasingly seems that people have grown tired of caring about these things, or even pretending to."

"In a world where evil corporations are everywhere and it feels like everyone is in some way complicit, there’s a tendency to dismiss the most blatant hypocrisies as the cost of doing business, and the cult of fandom—which has been encouraged by the music industry and social media platforms alike—regularly insulates artists (and those they work with) from any sort of accountability, especially when it comes to collaborating with corporate interests."

"The term “underground” now feels like less of a material reality and more of an empty aesthetic, to a point where artists and fans will enthusiastically don Underground Resistance t-shirts and pontificate about the marginalized origins of dance music, only to toss those morals out the window and look the other way when it comes to parties and festivals with ties to right-wing billionaires and some of the most noxious businesses on the planet."

+ The Corporations Are More Patient Than the Cool Kids "Superstruct is back in the news. According to reports by IQ and The Telegraph, the UK festival conglomerate—which is partially owned by KKR, a private equity giant with ties to to fossil fuel extraction, weapons manufacturing, surveillance technology and businesses that operate in Israel and the occupied West Bank—saw its losses and debt climb significantly during 2025, the year that the company was hit with widespread boycotts targeting Boiler Room, Sónar, Field Day, Mighty Hoopla and a litany of other events in its portfolio."

"It’s a bit early for the anti-KKR crowd to start celebrating, and it’s too early to declare that the boycott “worked”—or that it shouldn’t be continued and strengthened as Superstruct gears up for the 2027 festival season."

"Companies like this bank on even the most ardent critics gradually getting bored and moving on, and considering the microscopic attention span of the general public when it comes to music industry machinations, it likely falls on people who are actually in the music industry to stand up and push back against the continued incursion of private equity and corporate multinationals into the sector."
+ Enshittification +

+ The state of listening doesn't make headlines [Liz Pelly's newsletter] "I don’t know if there’s any modern music development that reflects the fraught state of listening today as well as the generative A.I. music apps. Listening has increasingly atomized and fractured, in-part thanks to how recorded sound is distributed and delivered today.

If streaming services helped shape context-less, self-oriented, vibe-based listening modes—“make the music revolve around me” is how I’ve described it in the past—then the A.I. apps take the same orientation and bring it to audio-generation, for the playlist-trained user who does not understand music as a form of communication and a site of curiosity but rather a backdrop to be controlled. Something they don’t have to listen to at all."

+ The long history of slop in music [The term might be everywhere now, but the act of copying and enshittifying has been around for decades, explains Ian McQuaid] [Huck] "I would argue that slop as we understand it in a musical context is any work that blandly, emotionlessly regurgitates other work whilst stripping out elements that might be unexpected or transgressive, leaving the thinnest gruel, whatever tiny sliver of sound that can be used to extract profit. Does it really make that much difference if the slop was made by an algorithm? AI may have democratised slop farming, it didn’t start it."

+ Experience Machine [On the queasy merger of art theater and the experience economy] [Artforum] "The better end of these stagings are exciting, lo-fi, and figure into a fun night out. Yet the experience of attending one of these shows often feels more akin to being “at” something than “seeing” something. Many of the stagings seem like a more engrossing, more elaborate, more laudable-for-effort version of the dreaded “reading” event model, whose main function is the temporary assembling of names that can permanently live on Instagram.

Now the dominant consumer relation, at least in the West, it commingles with and exchanges energies with the cultural field through “brand activations” disguised as cultural events, like a reading with a corporate sponsor or Charli XCX hosting a “conversation series” in New York City."

+ Photography +

+ The Unsung Legend of New York Gay Photography [Stanley Stellar wandered the streets of downtown, capturing gay men in moments of ecstasy and liberation. A new book, ‘Don’t Worry Boys,’ honors his iconic work] [i-D] "From the 1970s onwards, Stanley Stellar made photographs about sex and street culture in the streets of New York. Boys gathering in ass-hugging jeans on the sidewalk, naked bodies in clubs and cruising spots. On occasion, these men—many of whom he knew—would step into his studio, kiss each other, and present his camera with an erection. They are unique pictures of a time period when the gay community went through vast changes—starting shortly after Stonewall, through the AIDS crisis, into the ’90s—but they’re defined not by chillyness or melancholy, but by a cheekiness, a resilience, a pursuit of real liberation."

+ NYC GO-GO 2.0 [Slava Mogutin] [SoHo Project Space] [On view until 11/1] "I began documenting New York’s go-go scene in the early 2000s, by chance. My boyfriend at the time, Brian Kenny, worked in nightlife as a bartender and dancer, and I often found myself in the bars and clubs where he worked. I started photographing almost instinctively, drawn to the dancers—their stage personas, confidence, vulnerability, and unapologetic presence—and to the particular intimacy of these spaces. The pictures were primarily taken at The Cock and the now-defunct East Village bars Boysroom, Mr. Black, and Eastern Bloc, at a moment when downtown nightlife was already being ravaged by rising rents, gentrification, and increasing regulation."

+ Berlin +

+ When Berghain Was Political [Heist] "Having opened in once-punky Friedrichshain, Berghain now sits uneasily beside the Amazon tower, the Uber Eats Arena, the East Side Mall and an edgy corporate development complex using the club to flaunt its vision. No longer unimpeachably countercultural, the club now feels more like the adult Disneyland of Berlin."

"Berghain in 2026 shows us a gentrified Berlin, in which techno is the new EDM, a consumerist lifestyle you can buy into with off-the-rack costumes and TikTok techno dances. Instagram is the LinkedIn of the scene. It’s where superstar DJs regale you with origin stories suspiciously similar in format to those of corporate leaders, and where other DJs give self-help-book-style motivational speeches."

"It’s open season for techno influencers like Das Techno Team, who will teach you exactly how to dance to techno, and for the promoter BCCO, who posts fake online underground raves for aspiring baby clubbers. You will still have to go onto the real LinkedIn, though, to find out about classy techno dinner parties."

"Many among the new young left in Berlin are the children of Black and brown people. They protest the Gaza genocide, and they likely do not approve when the club's superstar resident DJ Ben Klock shares Instagram stories hanging out with the comedian Daniel-Ryan Spaulding."

+ Exploring the Fine Art of Public Sex with Jordan Tannahill and AA Bronson [Butt] BRONSON: Your twenties and your thirties you feel like that’s your real adult life, and once you’re in your forties you’re into old age. But actually, in my experience, life kind of begins at fifty and doesn’t really get going until sixty. And the stuff in your twenties and thirties is just part of your education, in a way. Turning eighty is like staring death in the face. You turn a corner, and you’ve got this death mask looking you in the eyes. It’s a very different experience, it’s so weird. I have no peers my age, not here anyway. And the couple I do have in Toronto are all women, no men, they’re all dead. I don’t know anyone my own age, and I don’t have any friends my own age who I would hang out with.

+ R.I.P. Grace Detrevarah [Queer Happened Here] "A self-described “Black Woman of Transgender Experience”, Grace Detrevarah (1962-2026) was a trans advocate-activist, & long-term HIV survivor whose collective work connected trans liberation with healthcare, housing, & the rights of incarcerated people."

+ R.I.P. Ray Caviano [QHH] "Raised in Manhattan’s Little Italy, the openly gay record executive played a significant role in turning the music of gay dance floors into national hits. At NYC’s Paradise Garage, Caviano brought test pressings directly to Larry Levan. He also brought WBLS’ Frankie Crocker to the club, recalling that records Levan played could reach radio the following morning. DJs including Levan & Danny Krivit often visited his office to assess new music, & these relationships helped carry music championed by the Garage’s largely Black & Latino gay crowd to a wider audience.

Reflecting on disco in 1993, Caviano said: “With disco, you were not an observer, you were a participant. You weren’t going to the party, you were the party.”
